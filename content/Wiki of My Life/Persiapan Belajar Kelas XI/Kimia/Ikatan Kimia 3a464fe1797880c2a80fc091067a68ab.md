# They are based on two rules to get stability

The **octet rule** and **duplet rule** explain how atoms become stable by achieving a full outermost electron shell.

### 1. Octet Rule

The **octet rule** states that **atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons so that they have 8 electrons in their outermost shell**, like the noble gases.

**Examples:**

- **Sodium (Na)** has 1 valence electron. It loses 1 electron to become stable.
- **Chlorine (Cl)** has 7 valence electrons. It gains 1 electron to complete its octet.
- **Oxygen (O)** has 6 valence electrons. It shares or gains 2 electrons to achieve 8.

### 2. Duplet Rule

The **duplet rule** applies to atoms whose first (K) shell can hold only **2 electrons**. Such atoms become stable when they have **2 electrons in their outermost shell**.

**Examples:**

- **Hydrogen (H)** has 1 electron. It shares or gains 1 electron to have 2 electrons.
- **Helium (He)** already has 2 electrons, so it is naturally stable.

### Summary

| Rule | Stable Outer Shell | Applies To |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Octet Rule** | 8 electrons | Most elements |
| **Duplet Rule** | 2 electrons | Hydrogen and helium |

**Easy way to remember:**

- **Octet = 8 electrons = Most atoms**
- **Duplet = 2 electrons = Hydrogen and helium**

## Ionic Bonds and Ionic Compounds as its results

Here's a simple explanation:

### Ionic Bond

An **ionic bond** is a bond formed when **one atom gives electrons to another atom**.

- The atom that **loses electrons** becomes a **positive ion**.
- The atom that **gains electrons** becomes a **negative ion**.
- The positive and negative ions **attract each other**, forming an ionic bond.

**Example: Sodium chloride (NaCl)**

- Sodium (Na) has **1 valence electron**.
- Chlorine (Cl) has **7 valence electrons**.
- Sodium gives its 1 electron to chlorine.
- Sodium becomes **Na⁺** (positive ion).
- Chlorine becomes **Cl⁻** (negative ion).
- Na⁺ and Cl⁻ attract each other, forming an **ionic bond**.

### Ionic Compound

An **ionic compound** is a **substance made up of positive and negative ions held together by ionic bonds**.

**Examples:**

- Sodium chloride (NaCl) – table salt
- Magnesium oxide (MgO)
- Calcium chloride (CaCl₂)

### Key Points

- **Ionic bond** = the **force of attraction** between positive and negative ions.
- **Ionic compound** = the **compound formed** by those ions.

### Easy way to remember

- **Bond** = the **glue** holding ions together.
- **Compound** = the **finished product** made from those ions.

**Analogy:** Imagine one person gives a ball to another.

- The giver becomes **positive** (lost something).
- The receiver becomes **negative** (gained something).
- Because they now have opposite "charges," they stick together. That "sticking together" is the **ionic bond**, and the pair together is the **ionic compound**.

## Naming Ions and Ionides

## Cations and anions

When
 a neutral atom loses one or more electrons, the total number of 
electrons decreases while the number of protons in the nucleus remains 
the same. The result is that the atom becomes a **cation**—an ion with a net *positive* charge. For example, the element sodium loses one electron to become a cation:

| Element name | Symbol | Protons | Electrons | Charge |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| sodium | ‍\[\ce{Na}\] | ‍\[11\] | ‍\[11\] | ‍\[0\] |
| sodium ion | ‍\[\ce{Na+}\] | ‍\[11\] | ‍\[10\] | ‍\[1+\] |

Similarly, when a neutral atom gains one or more electrons, the result is that the atom becomes an **anion**—an ion with a net *negative* charge. For example, the element chlorine gains one electron to become an anion:

| Element name | Symbol | Protons | Electrons | Charge |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| chlorine | ‍\[\ce{Cl}\] | ‍\[17\] | ‍\[17\] | ‍\[0\] |
| chloride ion | ‍\[\ce{Cl-}\] | ‍\[17\] | ‍\[18\] | ‍\[1-\] |

Main group elements (the elements in groups 1, 2, and 13-18 of the periodic 
table) typically form ions of only one charge. The diagram below shows 
the common charge of ions in different groups.

## Naming cations

How do we name main group cations? In general, the cation name is the same 
as the element name, but with the word "ion" added to the end.

For example, the alkali metals in group 1 on the periodic table tend to form cations with a ‍+1 charge. The following table shows how we name the ions for the first four elements in this group:

| Element | Ion symbol | Ion name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| hydrogen | $‍\ce{H+}$ | hydrogen ion |
| lithium | $‍\ce{Li+}$ | lithium ion |
| sodium | $‍\ce{Na+}$ | sodium ion |
| potassium | $‍\ce{K+}$ | potassium ion |

In speech, we may also refer to a hydrogen cation simply as  "‍H-plus". Similarly, a sodium cation can be called "a sodium ion" or "‍Na-plus".

The same rule applies to all other elements that typically form cations of 
one particular charge. This includes the alkaline earth metals in group 2
 and a few other metals you may be familiar with, shown in the table 
below:

| Element | Ion symbol | Ion name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| magnesium | $‍\ce{Mg^2+}$ | magnesium ion |
| calcium | $‍\ce{Ca^2+}$ | calcium ion |
| strontium | $‍\ce{Sr^2+}$ | strontium ion |
| aluminum | $‍\ce{Al^3+}$ | aluminum ion |
| silver | $‍\ce{Ag+}$ | silver ion |

Why don't we need to include a charge in the names of these ions? For 
example, why can we say "calcium ion" instead of "calcium-two-plus ion"?
 Since these main group elements usually form ions of only one charge, 
which can be predicted from the periodic table, the charge is implied.

## Naming anions

To name main group anions, we take the root of the element's name and add -*ide* to the end. The following table shows how this suffix is used to name anions of various elements:

| Element name | Ion name | Ion formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| hydrogen | **hydride** | $‍\ce{H}^-$ |
| chlorine | **chloride** | $‍\ce{Cl}^-$ |
| bromine | **bromide** | $‍\ce{Br}^-$ |
| iodine | **iodide** | ‍\[\ce{I}^-\] |
| oxygen | **oxide** | ‍\[\ce{O}^{2-}\] |
| sulfur | **sulfide** | ‍\[\ce{S}^{2-}\] |
| nitrogen | **nitride** | ‍\[\ce{N}^{3-}\] |
| phosphorus | **phosphide** | ‍\[\ce{P}^{3-}\] |
| carbon | **carbide** | ‍\[\ce{C}^{4-}\] |

Let's use the oxygen anion as an example. In writing, it is shown as the symbol $\ce{O}^{2-}$or written as "oxide". In speech, we'd typically refer to it as an "oxygen ion", "oxide", or “$\ce{O}$-two-minus"

Since
 we can predict the charge of main group anions by their location on the
 periodic table, it's usually not necessary to specify their charge in 
the name. For example, it is implied that an oxide ion has a -2 charge. We don't need to say "oxide-two-minus".

## Formulas and naming of basic ionic compounds

Now that we've seen the naming conventions for cations and anions, we can 
discuss how they apply to naming simple ionic compounds (a.k.a. *salts*). The following guidelines are used for naming ionic compounds:

- Always name the cation before the anion. The cation will appear before the anion in the chemical formula, too.
- Any ionic compound will have a net charge of zero. Another way of saying
this is that cations and anions must always combine in such a way so
that their charges cancel.
- The
number of cations and anions in the formula should be written as the
lowest possible integer value. For example, the formula for sodium
chloride is $\ce{NaCl}$, not $\ce{Na}_2 \ce{Cl}_2$ or some other multiple of $\ce{NaCl}$, even though the charges would still add up to zero.

Let's look at a few examples:

### What about more than one cations?

So far, we've considered elements that typically form cations of one 
particular charge. For example, the alkali metals and the alkaline earth
 metals usually form 1+ ions and 2+ ions, respectively.

Most transition metals and several metals in groups 13-17, however, can form
 cations of various charges. For instance, iron is often found as both 
the $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ and$\text{Fe}^{3+}$cations, and sometimes other charges as well. Thus, iron is **polyvalent**, which literally means "many valued"—it is able to form cations of different charges.

For metals that are polyvalent, we need to specify the magnitude of the 
charge on the ion. We do this by adding Roman numerals to the name, 
which indicate the charge of the cation. For example,  $‍\text{Fe}^{2+}$ is written as "iron(II)", and ‍\$\text{Fe}^{3+}$ is written as "iron(III)."

In speech, we have to call  $‍\text{Fe}^{2+}$ "iron two-plus" or "iron two" because simply referring to it as an "iron ion" will not give enough information to specify the cation 
charge.

## Example 1: Finding the chemical formula from the name

**What is the chemical formula of potassium chloride?**

Potassium ($\ce{K}$) is in group 1, so it forms a cation with a 1+ charge. Chloride is, by definition, an anion formed from an atom of chlorine ($\ce{Cl}$) . Chlorine is in group 17, so chloride has a ‍-1 charge. The potassium and chloride ions have equal and opposite charges, so they will join in a 1:1 ratio, forming $\ce{KCl}$.

(Remember that subscripts are not used when there is only one atom/ion of a particular type.)

## Example 2: Finding the name from the chemical formula

**What is the name of the ionic compound $\ce{Mg}_3\ce{P}_2$?**

Magnesium $(‍\ce{Mg})$ is in group 2, so it forms a cation with a ‍+2 charge. Because it is the cation in the compound, we put it first in the compound’s name and refer to it as just *magnesium*. (Notice we remove the word "ion" since it is now part of a compound).

Phosphorus $(‍\ce{P})$ is in group 15, so it forms anions with a ‍-3 charge. Because it is the anion in the compound, we put it second in the compound’s name and refer to the anion by its name, *phosphide*.

Therefore, the name for the ionic compound $\ce{Mg}_3\ce{P}_2$ is *magnesium phosphide*.

### What about polyvalents?

If an element can form more than one type of cation, we have to specify 
the charge on that cation. The magnitude of the charge for a transition 
metal cation is usually indicated using Roman numerals in parentheses 
after the name of the metal—this is also called the **systematic name** of the ion. The following table lists some of the most common ions for polyvalent metals.

For some ions, the common name is also given. The common names are somewhat
 old fashioned nowadays, but they're still used in some cases. Notice 
that the ions of lesser charge take the suffix *-ous* in the common name; ions of higher charge take the *-ic* suffix.

For example, ferr*ous* chloride (‍\[\ce{FeCl}_2\]) is the common name of iron(II) chloride and contains the ‍\[\text{Fe}^{2+}\] ion. Ferr*ic* chloride (‍\[\ce{FeCl}_3\]) is the common name for iron(III) chloride and contains the ‍\[\text{Fe}^{3+}\] ion.

| Element | Common ions formed | Systematic name | Common name |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| chromium | $‍\ce{Cr}^{2+}$ | chromium(II) | chromous |
|  | $‍\ce{Cr}^{3+}$ | chromium(III) | chromic |
| cobalt | $‍\ce{Co}^{2+}$ | cobalt(II) |  |
|  | $‍\ce{Co}^{3+}$ | cobalt(III) |  |
| copper | $‍\ce{Cu}^{+}$ | copper(I) | cuprous |
|  | $‍\ce{Cu}^{2+}$ | copper(II) | cupric |
| iron | $‍\ce{Fe}^{2+}$ | iron(II) | ferrous |
|  | $‍\ce{Fe}^{3+}$ | iron(III) | ferric |
| lead | $‍\ce{Pb}^{2+}$ | lead(II) |  |
|  | $‍\ce{Pb}^{4+}$ | lead(IV) |  |
| tin | $‍\ce{Sn}^{2+}$ | tin(II) | stannous |
|  | $‍\ce{Sn}^{4+}$ | tin(IV) | stannic |

*Note:
 Roman numerals are only needed for polyvalent elements. Elements which 
form ions of only one charge do not require Roman numerals.*

The following transition metals (groups 3-12) do *not* require a Roman numeral in their name since they only form one cation:

| Element | Ion symbol | Systematic name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| scandium | $‍\ce{Sc^3+}$ | scandium |

Using this table as a reference, let's practice naming an ionic compound containing a polyvalent metal.

**What is the name of the compound  $‍\ce{PbCl}_4$?**

When
 naming ionic compounds that contain transition metals, we first need to
 determine the charge on the transition metal cation. We can deduce this
 charge by first calculating the charge contributed by the anion, whose 
charge we already know for sure.

We recognize that $\ce{Cl}$ is a group 17 halogen, so it forms the chloride anion $\ce{Cl}^-$. We can see from the chemical formula $\ce{PbCl}_4$
 that there are four chloride ions in the compound. The total negative 
charge contributed by the four chloride ions is calculated below:

$$
‍\text{Total charge from anions}=4\times(-1)=-4
$$

In order for the compound to be electrically neutral, the lead cation must be $\ce{Pb}^{4+}$. This is because the ‍+4 charge on this ion will exactly cancel the net -4 charge contributed by the four chloride ions.

Therefore, the name of $\ce{PbCl}$ is *lead(IV) chloride*.

## Conclusion

Cations  are positively charged ions formed when neutral atoms lose electrons; 
anions are negatively charged ions formed when neutral atoms gain 
electrons. It is possible to predict the charges of main group ions by 
looking at the group numbers on the periodic table.

To name main group cations, we simply refer to them as the element's name +
 "ion". To name main group anions, we add the suffix -ide to the end of 
the element's name.

Cations and anions combine to form ionic compounds. Ionic compounds are named 
with the cation first and the anion last. The same convention is used 
when writing their chemical formulas. Ionic compounds must be 
electrically neutral. Therefore, the cations and anions combine in such a
 way that the net charge contributed by the total number of cations 
exactly cancels the net charge contributed by the total number of 
anions.

In chemistry, "Group 3-12" refers to the **transition metals** on the periodic table. Stating they have **variable charges** means **these elements can lose different numbers of electrons depending on the chemical reaction, allowing the exact same element to form ions with different positive charges** (e.g., Iron can have a $(+2) \space or \space (+3)$ charge). ## Bonding with polyatomic ions

Like monatomic ions, polyatomic ions participate in **ionic bonds** with other ions (but remember, the atoms *within* a polyatomic ion molecule are held together by **covalent bonds**, which we will cover in more detail in the next lesson).

Polyatomic ions combine with other ions in a ratio that produces a *neutral* compound overall. Subscripts are used to indicate the number of ions needed to make that neutral compound.

If you need more than one polyatomic ion, enclose the ion in parentheses and add the subscript *outside* of the parentheses.

For example, magnesium ‍\[\ce{(Mg)}\] loses two electrons to form a ‍\[2+\] cation. When magnesium bonds with hydroxide ‍\[\ce{(OH^-)}\], *two* hydroxide ions are needed for every magnesium ion in order to form a neutral compound.

So, the formula for magnesium hydroxide is ‍\[\boxed{\ce{Mg(OH)2}}\].

### Example 1

*Potassium sulfate*
 consists of potassium ions and sulfate ions bonded together. Potassium 
is an element in group 1 of the periodic table. Therefore, we know that 
potassium forms *monatomic* ions with charge ‍\[1+\]. Sulfate is a *polyatomic* ion. We can see in the table above that sulfate ions have a charge of ‍\[2-\].

To produce a neutral compound, two ‍\[\ce{K}^+\] are needed for every ‍\[\ce{SO_4}^{2-}\]. So, the formula for potassium sulfate is ‍\[\boxed{\ce{K_2SO_4}}\].

The ‍\[\ce{K}^+\] and ‍\[\ce{SO_4}^{2-}\] ions are held together by *ionic* bonds. The ‍\[\ce{S}\] and ‍\[\ce{O}\] atoms within each ‍\[\ce{SO_4}^{2-}\] are held together by *covalent* bonds.

### Example 2

*Strontium nitrate*
 is composed of strontium ions and nitrate ions bonded together. 
Strontium is an element in group 2 of the periodic table; it forms 
monatomic ‍\[\ce{Sr}^{2+}\] ions. Nitrate is a polyatomic ion with formula ‍\[\ce{NO_3}^-\].

To produce a neutral compound, one ‍\[\ce{Sr}^{2+}\] is needed for every two ‍\[\ce{NO_3}^-\]. So, the formula for strontium nitrate is ‍\[\boxed{\ce{Sr(NO_3)_2}}\].

*Note how parentheses are added around the polyatomic ion. This lets us indicate that the subscript ‍\[2\] applies to the entire nitrate unit.*

## Covalent vs Ionic Bonds

To distinguish between ionic and covalent bonds quickly, rely on the **Element Type Rule**:

> **Metal + Non-Metal = Ionic Bond** *(Electrons are transferred)*
> 
> 
> **Non-Metal + Non-Metal = Covalent Bond** *(Electrons are shared)*
> 

## Underlying Mechanisms

### 1. Ionic Bonds (Transfer of Electrons)

- **What happens:** The metal atom completely loses one or more valence electrons to the non-metal atom.
- **Why it happens:** Metals have low electronegativity (weak hold on electrons) and low ionization energy, whereas non-metals have high electronegativity (strong pull for electrons).
- **The result:** The loss and gain of electrons produce oppositely charged ions (a positive cation and a negative anion) that are drawn together by strong electrostatic attraction.
- **Example:** Sodium ($\text{Na}$, a metal) transfers an electron to Chlorine ($\text{Cl}$, a non-metal) to form table salt ($\text{NaCl}$).

### 2. Covalent Bonds (Sharing of Electrons)

- **What happens:** Two atoms share pairs of valence electrons.
- **Why it happens:** Both non-metals have high electronegativity, meaning neither atom can fully strip an electron away from the other. To achieve a stable octet (full outer shell), they overlap their atomic orbitals.
- **The result:** The shared electron pair holds the two nuclei together.
- **Example:** Oxygen ($\text{O}$) and Hydrogen ($\text{H}$), both non-metals, share electrons to form water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$).

## Comparison Summary

| **Property** | **Ionic Bonds** | **Covalent Bonds** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Participating Elements** | Metal + Non-metal | Non-metal + Non-metal |
| **Electron Behavior** | Transferred | Shared |
| **Electronegativity Difference** | High difference | Low to moderate difference |
| **Physical State at Room Temp** | Crystalline solids | Gases, liquids, or soft solids |
| **Melting / Boiling Points** | High | Generally low |
| **Electrical Conductivity** | Conducts when molten or dissolved in water | Generally poor conductors |
